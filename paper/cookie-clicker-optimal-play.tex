\begin{abstract}
We study purchase order, banking, heavenly upgrades, and ascension in \emph{Cookie Clicker}. The requested objective is minimum expected completion time from a new save. Completion includes all normal achievements, upgrades, and unlocks. Shadow achievements and specified long sugar-lump time gates are excluded.

The existing autoplay policy does not have a proof of this objective. We give a strict counterexample to its greedy purchase rule, even with constant additive production. We correct achievement valuation, heavenly-block scheduling, and the ascension planner's missing no-reset action. We also correct two execution defects found in the installed game source.

A finite purchase model admits an exact completion policy by dynamic programming. The accompanying Lean project checks explicitly stated model results and a completion predicate generated from the pinned Steam game. An isolated adapter executes the installed game source. Its legal action coverage and its correspondence to Lean are not complete. The remaining gameplay policies are heuristics. Historical simulation results are retained as unverified reports, not certificates of optimality.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{MSC 2020:} 90C39 (primary); 60G40, 90B35 (secondary).\\
\textit{Keywords:} incremental games, optimal stopping, dynamic programming, exchange arguments, scheduling.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%======================================================================
\emph{Cookie Clicker}~\cite{orteil} is an incremental game. Clicks and buildings produce cookies. Each copy of a building costs $1.15$ times the previous copy. Upgrades multiply production, and random \emph{golden cookies} give temporary bonuses.

At any time the player can \emph{ascend}. Ascension abandons the current run. In return the player gains \emph{prestige}, a permanent production multiplier that grows like the cube root of all cookies ever baked. Prestige also gives \emph{heavenly chips}, which buy permanent upgrades.

Four decisions recur in play: purchases, golden-cookie banking, heavenly upgrades, and ascension. This paper separates exact model results from gameplay estimates. The audit of 29 October 2026 corrects claims that exceeded their assumptions.

\subsection{The four rules}\label{sec:rules}
Let $c$ be the income rate. Item $i$ costs $p_i$ and adds $\Delta_i$ to the income.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Two-purchase order} (Theorem~\ref{thm:exchange}). For two required purchases with neither affordable, compare the index
\[
 E_i=\frac{p_i}{c}+\frac{p_i}{\Delta_i}.
\]
\item \emph{The \Lucky{} bank} (Proposition~\ref{prop:bank}). The frozen marginal-income comparison favours item $i$ if and only if
\[
 \frac{p_i}{\bar\Delta_i}<\theta=\frac1{0.15\,g\,\lambda_L}.
\]
Here $\bar\Delta_i$ is the full income gain of the item, $g$ multiplies golden-cookie gains and $\lambda_L$ is the rate of \Lucky{} cookies.
\item \emph{Heavenly chips} (Theorem~\ref{thm:smith}). Assume constant chip effort, no initial chip bank, and no parent constraints. Upgrade $j$ costs $p_j$ chips and multiplies speed by $m_j$. To acquire a fixed set, use decreasing order of
\[
 \rho_j=\frac{1-1/m_j}{p_j}.
\]
\item \emph{Ascension} (Corollary~\ref{cor:stop}). In the power-law model with zero reset overhead, a step attaining the lower bound uses the root $v^\star>1$ of
\[
 \frac\beta3\Bigl(1-\frac1v\Bigr)=\frac{1-v^{-\gamma/3}}\gamma,\qquad\gamma=\frac3\beta-1,
\]
where $\beta$ is the growth exponent of the current run.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:rest} adds rules for the other systems: achievements, Godzamok, wrinklers, the minigames, the dragon and seasons. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} reports historical simulator results whose original traces are unavailable.

\subsection{Scope and conventions}\label{sec:conventions}
A local income comparison does not prove minimum completion time. The word ``optimal'' below refers only to the model and objective stated with each result. Cookie~Monster+~\cite{cmplus} combines these comparisons with estimated values and execution rules. It is not a certified solution to the full completion problem.

\subsection{The completion objective}\label{sec:completion-objective}
Let $s_0$ be a new save, let $\pi$ be a legal policy, and let $\mathcal T$ be the completion set. The requested objective is
\[
 \inf_\pi \E_{s_0}^{\pi}[\tau_{\mathcal T}],\qquad
 \tau_{\mathcal T}=\inf\{t\ge0:s_t\in\mathcal T\}.
\]
The set $\mathcal T$ requires all normal achievements, upgrades, and finite unlocks under the versioned profile in \texttt{model/completion-profile.json}. Shadow achievements are excluded. Completion cannot mean merely ``nothing is affordable now''. Repeated building purchases and levels are not terminal requirements in their own right.

The initialized Steam 2.053 catalogue contains 622 normal achievements, 17 shadow achievements, four unused dungeon achievements, and 875 upgrades. The profile excludes 24 individual sugar achievements: the 20 building-level achievements, the 365-lump total, and the normal bifurcated, meaty, and caramelized lump achievements. All other 598 normal achievements remain required. The profile retains lump costs for ordinary progression. Cursor level 12, farm level 9, and the four minigame unlocks still apply.

The terminal predicate requires current ownership of all 845 legal non-toggle upgrades, including heavenly, cosmetic, and offline upgrades. It requires access to all 17 toggles and selectors; matching-version acquisition history can satisfy mutually exclusive choices. All 34 Garden seeds must currently be unlocked. Thus the required Garden sacrifice must be followed by a complete seed log again. The final dragon, Santa, office, plot, soil, spell, god, good, and loan unlocks also count. The final state must be in a normal run, outside ascension and reincarnation transitions.

Completion count readers now admit every represented nonnegative finite integer, including values at and above $2^{53}$. Loss of unit-increment precision does not invalidate a native threshold comparison. Lean checks the count conversion at $2^{53}$, $2^{1000}$ and the largest finite binary64 integer, $2^{1024}-2^{971}$. JSON observations use binary64 decimal conversion before the count check; nonfinite, negative and fractional counts remain invalid. These reader results do not assert that all supplied large-count fixtures are reachable. The source-heap reader also rejects catalogue descriptors explicitly marked unresolved, including owned upgrades, spells and gods. It does not replace an unknown descriptor with a recorded placeholder.

Every milk choice is a required unlock. Hazelnut milk requires 600 normal achievements. The 598 specified achievements and the achievement implied by cursor level 12 supply only 599. At least one other excluded sugar achievement must therefore be won, unless it has already been obtained. This does not require all 24 excluded achievements. It preserves a separate finite unlock dependency. Shadow achievements do not contribute to this count. The predicate also requires the actual native \texttt{AchievementsOwned} counter to reach 600, because the milk selector reads that counter. It does not infer the counter from won flags. A source callback can set a flag before later notification work and the counter increment; an interrupted callback can therefore leave the two values different.

The executable predicate reports every missing condition. It does not prove that an arbitrary supplied state or acquisition history is reachable. The current mod omits several required achievement and seed paths and still uses income forecasts. Random events need state-dependent transition probabilities; the aggregate outcome shares in this paper do not supply them. A complete proof also needs an attaining policy and a matching expected-time lower bound. This audit does not supply that full-game certificate.

The user permits scheduled maintenance reloads that save the current state and then reload it. The user excludes earlier-save restoration, save/reload outcome retries, and deliberate random-outcome manipulation through native UI actions. The user also permits reading and decoding the current save shown by native Export save, including the startup seed. Native export and save bookkeeping effects remain part of the transition. This does not permit import, editing, restoration, or retries. The user forbids timing chosen to change seeded outcomes, including harvest timestamps selected using the exported seed. This restriction concerns the decision rule: the same timestamp can result from an ordinary schedule or a seed search, so a trace alone cannot distinguish them. The strategy must not change the clock or inspect future free draws or generator state that the native public interface does not reveal. Diagnostic fixtures used to audit such behavior are not admissible strategies. These restrictions do not remove the need to specify the transition law under permitted observations.

The selected mathematical environment supplies independent uniform free draws on $\{k/2^{53}:0\leq k<2^{53}\}$. Explicitly seeded generators retain the pinned native algorithm, seed construction, and draw order. A no-argument \texttt{Math.seedrandom()} returns to the independent free law; this is an environment idealization, not a statement about browser entropy. The clock is monotone and unbounded. Finite native Date range and eventual floating-point timestamp stagnation are therefore not completion obstructions in this mathematical model. The selected extension also processes arbitrary finite timestamp text without native string-limit failure or a stuck traversal index, while preserving native seeded results for ordinary timestamps. Ordinary economic arithmetic remains binary64. Source propagation of this extension remains a proof obligation. The executable experiments still use finite native dates and a reproducible sample generator. They do not implement the full selected probability space or unbounded calendar.

The user also fixes the herald count at 100, with no later external changes. This is an explicit environment assumption: the Steam source otherwise obtains player-count replies from an external service, whose future law is not specified by the game. The isolated adapter already supplies a constant reply that the native callback maps to 100 heralds; policy evaluation now rejects another count. The source callback and its scheduling still require formal refinement.

Times are in seconds unless a unit is given. All logarithms are natural. The game runs at 30 frames per second. A \emph{run} is the period between two ascensions, and \emph{CpS} means cookies per second from buildings.

\subsection{A retained time gate and controllable random state}\label{sec:source-randomness}
Hand-picked remains required. Its source branch requires a mature but unripe natural lump. With all permitted maturity reductions, the maturity threshold in milliseconds is
\[
 d=\frac{20\cdot3600000-3600000-7\cdot60000}{1+0.05\cdot1.1}
   =\frac{13\,596\,000\,000}{211}.
\]
The reductions are Diabetica Daemonicus, Ichor syrup, Dragon's Curve, and Reality Bending. The integer clock must therefore advance at least $\lceil d\rceil=64\,436\,019$ milliseconds from the lump's start before that branch is eligible. This is about 17 hours 54 minutes. It omits the time needed to unlock the lump and obtain the reductions. Lean checks the rational arithmetic and integer threshold. Native-engine tests check the corresponding numeric boundary. These are separate checks; they do not prove a refinement of the whole engine.

A tempting expected-time bound is $2d$, obtained by treating every attempt as a fresh fair coin and restarting a full growth interval after failure. That conclusion needs additional assumptions. Ordinary save rollback can undo an unpersisted failed attempt. More subtly, the You building's clone-hover renderer seeds the global generator for a clone name and does not restore it before returning. A later harvest can therefore use the seeded stream. The audit reproduces this callback behavior in the pinned engine. Both forms of outcome manipulation are excluded from the requested strategy class. The diagnostic fixture is not an admissible new-save route, and excluding it still does not prove a conditional fair-coin law for the remaining controls.

The selected free law is more specific than ECMAScript, which specifies an approximately uniform random or pseudorandom result rather than an exact independent law~\cite{ecmascript}. The game's own seeded calls add observable dependencies. Replacing those seeded calls with independent draws would change the control problem. A source inventory accounts for 23 reseeding call sites in eight callback bodies. The Lean control-flow analysis includes branches, early returns, loops, and seven expanded source helpers. Unresolved calls have an unknown phase; they are not assumed to preserve it. Three extracted callback closures have no unresolved explicit calls. The forbidden clone-hover leak is detected. The source extractor, implicit exceptions, host operations, and the remaining callees still require justification before this is a whole-engine phase theorem.

Even the five-letter startup seed is not exactly uniform over the $26^5$ strings. The native expression is \texttt{floor(26*Math.random())}, with a binary64 product. Put $q=346\,430\,740\,566\,961$. The letters a, f, k, o, u, and y each have $q+1$ of the $2^{53}$ input indices; each other letter has $q$. Lean proves all 26 thresholds for an integer projection of that rounded product and the resulting five-letter product law. Native boundary comparisons and actual seed-generation calls agree. A uniform prior over seed strings would therefore change the selected probability model.

Public observations can reveal a seed without reading hidden state. The visible fortune numbers $(73,43,10,64)$ leave exactly two startup candidates, \texttt{aaodk} and \texttt{abeby}, in an exhaustive scan of all $11\,881\,376$ strings. Their prior weights differ. The visible Mice clicking mice tooltip then distinguishes these candidates by its seeded wording, ``blasphemouse'' or ``blasphemous''. The scan and its source hashes are archived. This is a finite inference witness, not a theorem that every seed is identifiable. A legal history-dependent policy must nevertheless be allowed to retain such information. Seeded spell outcomes cannot in general be replaced by fresh independent outcomes after public observations.

For finite adaptive trees built from the selected free kernel, Lean computes the two-item harvest choice probability as exactly $1/2$. Arbitrary intervening free observations and decisions to stop do not improve the probability of success: after at most $n$ harvest attempts, failure has probability at least $2^{-n}$. The theorem derives this inequality from the kernel instead of assuming a conditional-fairness field. Applying it to arbitrary game histories still requires the source-phase correspondence and the infinite-history probability construction.

A conditional lower bound can nevertheless be stated precisely. Let $D=64\,436\,019$ be the minimum integer spacing. Assume that Hand-picked is initially absent, each attempted unripe harvest has conditional success probability at most $1/2$, and every next eligible attempt requires at least $D$ milliseconds after a failed attempt. The first eligible attempt must also require at least $D$ milliseconds from the new save. If $T$ is the full completion time, with $T=\infty$ on unsuccessful paths, then for every integer $n\geq0$,
\[
  \mathbb{E}[T]\;\geq\;
  D\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}2^{-j}
  =2D(1-2^{-n}).
\]
The first $D$ integer survival terms are one. Each following block is bounded below by the probability of still lacking Hand-picked after the preceding attempts. The conditional failure bound gives at least $2^{-j}$ for block $j$. Taking the supremum over $n$ yields $\mathbb{E}[T]\geq128\,872\,038$ milliseconds, about 35.8 hours. This is a necessary bound under the stated bridge assumptions. It is not attained by any policy established here, and it omits other mandatory goals.

\subsection{A native zero-delay acquisition}
Dragon petting has no gameplay cooldown. The two-second check in \texttt{Game.ClickSpecialPic} limits a sound; every pet makes another free drop trial. At a fixed quarter-hour, the native seeded shuffle chooses the same item. If that item is known to be missing, the selected 53-bit law gives the exact trial probability
\[
 p=\frac{450\,359\,962\,737\,050}{2^{53}}
   =\frac{225\,179\,981\,368\,525}{4\,503\,599\,627\,370\,496}.
\]
This is slightly greater than $1/20$ because the native comparison uses the binary64 value of \texttt{1/20} and a finite uniform grid. After $n$ failures the remaining mass is $(1-p)^n>0$, and this mass tends to zero. With zero simulated input latency, retrying until the visible unlock occurs therefore attains zero additional elapsed time almost surely. The infinite-failure path retains hitting time $\infty$ and has probability zero. Lean checks the exact count, normalized first-success masses, hitting-time convention, and local minimum. This result concerns one known-missing quarter's item, not full-game completion.

The missing-item condition matters. A quarter can select an item already owned, in which case indefinite same-time retries do not help. The experimental controller learns quarter-to-item mappings only from observed new unlocks. It skips known completed quarters and uses positive waits when existing ownership makes the current quarter uncertain. The practical mod uses bounded batches to keep the interface responsive. Version 2.053.16 removes its former one-minute batch delay, stops on an observed unlock, and preserves the user's menu and volume.

\subsection{Mechanics}\label{sec:mechanics}
A run starts with no buildings. Building $k$ with $n$ copies costs
\begin{equation}\label{eq:price}
 p_k(n)=p_k(0)\,1.15^{\,n}.
\end{equation}
Let $A$ be the cookies baked in all previous runs, and $C$ the cookies baked in the current run. Ascension converts all-time cookies into the prestige
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prestige}
 P(A+C)=\Bigl(\frac{A+C}{10^{12}}\Bigr)^{1/3}.
\end{equation}

Each prestige level adds $h$ percent to production. The \emph{prestige power} $h$ becomes $1$ after the heavenly upgrade \emph{Legacy} (one chip) and five cheap store upgrades, \emph{Heavenly chip secret} through \emph{Heavenly key}. A run that starts at prestige $P$ thus has the production multiplier
\begin{equation}\label{eq:multiplier}
 M(P)=1+0.01\,h\,\floor{P}.
\end{equation}
The player receives $\lfloor P\rfloor$ heavenly chips over the whole game. Each heavenly upgrade has a price in chips and a set of parent upgrades, which must be owned first.

\subsection{Assumptions}\label{sec:assumptions}
\begin{assumption}\label{as:piecewise}
Between purchases the income rate is constant. Purchases are instantaneous.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}\label{as:additive}
The income that a purchase adds does not depend on the order of a given pair of purchases.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}\label{as:speed}
Permanent bonuses multiply every income source (buildings, clicks and golden-cookie rewards) by the same factor $S$.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}\label{as:scalefree}
Purchase decisions depend on the bank and the holdings only through comparisons that do not change when all incomes are multiplied by a constant.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}\label{as:power}
(Section~\ref{sec:late} only.) The run curve is a power law, $F(x)=\alpha x^\beta$ with $\beta>1$.
\end{assumption}

\section{Purchase order}\label{sec:purchase}
%======================================================================
\begin{theorem}[Pairwise exchange]\label{thm:exchange}
Assume \ref{as:piecewise} and \ref{as:additive}, with $c,p_A,p_B,\Delta_A,\Delta_B>0$. Two purchases $A$ and $B$ occur one after the other, from a bank $b\le\min(p_A,p_B)$ at income $c$. Buying $A$ first completes both purchases no later than buying $B$ first if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:index}
 E_A\le E_B,\qquad E_i=\frac{p_i}c+\frac{p_i}{\Delta_i}.
\end{equation}
Both orders end in the same state.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
With $A$ first, the bank reaches $p_A$ after $(p_A-b)/c$ seconds and is then empty. Purchase $B$ follows after a further $p_B/(c+\Delta_A)$ seconds. Thus
\[
 T_{AB}=\frac{p_A-b}c+\frac{p_B}{c+\Delta_A},\qquad T_{BA}=\frac{p_B-b}c+\frac{p_A}{c+\Delta_B}.
\]
The bank $b$ cancels in the difference, and
\begin{align*}
 T_{AB}\le T_{BA}
 &\iff p_A\Bigl(\frac1c-\frac1{c+\Delta_B}\Bigr)\le p_B\Bigl(\frac1c-\frac1{c+\Delta_A}\Bigr)\\
 &\iff \frac{p_A\,\Delta_B}{c+\Delta_B}\le\frac{p_B\,\Delta_A}{c+\Delta_A}\\
 &\iff \frac{p_A}c+\frac{p_A}{\Delta_A}\le\frac{p_B}c+\frac{p_B}{\Delta_B}.
\end{align*}
The last step multiplies by $(c+\Delta_A)(c+\Delta_B)/(c\,\Delta_A\Delta_B)>0$. After both purchases, the holdings, the empty bank and the income $c+\Delta_A+\Delta_B$ agree by \ref{as:additive}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:local}
Both orders end in the same state. Thus the faster order is preferable when these two purchases must come next. This proves an adjacent-pair comparison, not a globally optimal choice among three or more purchases. The index changes with income. The following counterexample has strict comparisons throughout.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[A strict counterexample to greedy completion]\label{ex:greedy}
Start with $b=0$ and $c=1$. Three required items have $(p,\Delta)$ equal to $A=(3,1)$, $B=(5,4)$, and $C=(6,6)$. Initially the indices are $6$, $25/4$, and $7$. Greedy buys $A$. At income $2$, the remaining indices are $15/4$ and $4$, so it buys $B$ next. Its completion time is
\[
 T_{ABC}=3+\frac52+\frac66=\frac{13}{2}.
\]
The order $B,C,A$ takes
\[
 T_{BCA}=5+\frac65+\frac3{11}=\frac{356}{55}
 =\frac{13}{2}-\frac3{110}.
\]
Both orders finish with the same holdings, an empty bank, and income $12$. Thus the greedy policy is slower under the paper's own assumptions.
\end{example}

\subsection{An exact finite completion model}\label{sec:finite-completion}
Fix a finite set $R$ of required, independent, single-use purchases. Prices are nonnegative and gains are nonnegative. Income $c$ is positive, the bank $b$ is nonnegative, and no random event or reset occurs. Completion means owning every item in $R$.

For each remaining item $i$, define the earliest waiting time and resulting bank by
\[
 w_i(b,c)=\frac{\max(p_i-b,0)}c,\qquad
 b_i=\max(b-p_i,0).
\]
The exact recursion is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:completion-dp}
 V(b,c,\varnothing)=0,\qquad
 V(b,c,R)=\min_{i\in R}\{w_i(b,c)+V(b_i,c+\Delta_i,R\setminus\{i\})\}.
\end{equation}
The selected item is followed by the policy selected in the resulting state.

\begin{theorem}[Finite completion optimum]\label{thm:completion}
Recursion~\eqref{eq:completion-dp} attains the least completion time among all purchase orders in this model, with each purchase made when affordable.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Induct on the number of remaining items. The empty state is complete. Every nonempty order has a first item $i$ and a suffix on $R\setminus\{i\}$. The induction hypothesis bounds the suffix below by its recursive value. Taking the minimum over the finite first choices gives a lower bound for every order. Choosing a minimising first item and an attaining suffix achieves that bound.
\end{proof}

There is no advantage to an extra delay in this deterministic model. For a fixed order, move the first delayed purchase to its earliest affordable time. At its former time, the bank is no smaller, since the purchase gain is nonnegative. The remaining purchases can occur at their former times or earlier. Repeating this operation removes every extra delay. This argument does not apply when a delay changes prices, unlocks, buffs, or random-event rewards.

For a purchased subset $S$, the bank after earliest purchases is $\max(b_0-\sum_{i\in S}p_i,0)$. Income is $c_0+\sum_{i\in S}\Delta_i$. Therefore a subset dynamic program uses $O(n2^n)$ transitions and $O(2^n)$ states. The reference implementation uses rational arithmetic. This theorem does not model repeatable buildings, achievement unlocks, or heavenly resets.

\begin{corollary}[Payback period]\label{cor:pp}
Cookie Monster ranks purchases by the payback period
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pp}
 \mathrm{PP}_i=\frac{\max(p_i-b,0)}c+\frac{p_i}{\Delta_i}.
\end{equation}
When no candidate is affordable, $\mathrm{PP}_i=E_i-b/c$. The payback period and the exchange index then give the same ranking.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proposition}[Affordable pairs]\label{prop:affordable}
If $\max(p_A,p_B)\le b<p_A+p_B$, buying $A$ first is better if and only if $\Delta_A>\Delta_B$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Buy $A$ at once. The bank keeps $b-p_A<p_B$, and the remaining $p_A+p_B-b$ cookies arrive at the rate $c+\Delta_A$:
\[
 T_{AB}=\frac{p_A+p_B-b}{c+\Delta_A}.
\]
The expression for $T_{BA}$ is symmetric, with the same numerator.
\end{proof}

With a large bank the exchange index is therefore not exact. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} shows that the effect is small. Over nine scenarios, the bank-independent index or a look-ahead to tier upgrades changes the time to a target by at most $0.35\%$ in continuous play. When purchases occur only once an hour, the change is at most $1.7\%$.

\section{Golden cookies and the bank}\label{sec:golden}

\subsection{When to hold a \texorpdfstring{\Lucky{}}{Lucky!} bank}\label{sec:bank}
A \Lucky{} golden cookie pays
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lucky}
 \min(0.15\,b,\ 900\,c)\,g+13
\end{equation}
cookies, where $g$ collects the golden-cookie gain multipliers. Below the cap, $b<6000\,c$, the reward is linear in the bank.

\begin{proposition}[Marginal value of banked cookies]\label{prop:bank}
Let \Lucky{} cookies occur at the rate $\lambda_L$, and let $b<6000\,c$. A marginal banked cookie earns $0.15\,g\,\lambda_L$ cookies per second in expectation. For a frozen income comparison, a divisible investment with yield $\bar\Delta_i/p_i$ has the larger marginal return if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta}
 \frac{p_i}{\bar\Delta_i}<\theta,\qquad\theta=\frac1{0.15\,g\,\lambda_L}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Below the cap, each \Lucky{} cookie pays $0.15\,g$ per banked cookie. These cookies arrive at the rate $\lambda_L$. By linearity of expectation, a banked cookie returns $0.15\,g\,\lambda_L$ per second. The assumed divisible investment returns $\bar\Delta_i/p_i$ per second. Compare these two instantaneous marginal returns.
\end{proof}

Actual purchases are indivisible. The inequality alone does not prove an optimal bank policy or a minimum completion time. Below the cap, bank rewards also change as the bank changes.

Without golden cookies or clicks, $\bar\Delta_i$ is the CpS gain $\Delta_i$. With both automated, $\Delta_i$ undervalues every purchase: golden cookies and clicks multiply a CpS gain about eightfold (Example~\ref{ex:theta}). With $\Delta_i$ alone, purchases whose payback lies between $\theta$ and several times $\theta$ would wait, although a purchase is better.

The rate is $\lambda_L=\pi_L/\E[I]$. Here $\pi_L\approx0.42$ is the probability that a golden cookie is \Lucky{}, and $I$ is the interval between golden cookies.

\begin{remark}[Golden-cookie interval approximation]\label{lem:interval}
Golden cookies spawn between $t_{\min}$ and $t_{\max}$. In each frame of the window, the spawn probability is $((t-t_{\min})/W)^5$, where $t$ is the current frame and the window lasts $W$ frames. Write $I=t_{\min}+x\,(t_{\max}-t_{\min})$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:interval}
 \E[x]\approx\Gamma\bigl(\tfrac76\bigr)\Bigl(\frac6W\Bigr)^{1/6}.
\end{equation}
\end{remark}

\noindent\emph{Derivation.}
The probability of surviving to the fraction $x$ of the window is
\[
 \prod_{k\le xW}\bigl(1-(k/W)^5\bigr)\approx\exp\Bigl(-W\int_0^xu^5\,du\Bigr)=\exp\bigl(-Wx^6/6\bigr).
\]
With $\int_0^\infty e^{-ax^n}\,dx=a^{-1/n}\,\Gamma(1+1/n)$,
\[
 \E[x]\approx\int_0^1e^{-Wx^6/6}\,dx\approx\int_0^\infty e^{-Wx^6/6}\,dx=\Bigl(\frac6W\Bigr)^{1/6}\Gamma\bigl(\tfrac76\bigr).
\]

\begin{definition}[Automation values]\label{def:auto}
Let $c$ be CpS and $k$ the income of an auto-clicker at $r$ clicks per second. A purchase adds $\Delta_i$ to $c$ and $r\,\delta_i$ to $k$, where $\delta_i$ is its gain in cookies per click, including click power that is a share of CpS. Table~\ref{tab:outcomes} lists the golden-cookie outcomes. Per unit of CpS and of click income, they add on average
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma}
 \sigma_c=\frac{(462\,\pi_F+3\bar n\,\pi_B+25200\,g\,\pi_S)\,d}{\E[I]},\qquad
 \sigma_k=\frac{(462\,\pi_F+3\bar n\,\pi_B+10088\,\pi_C)\,d}{\E[I]},
\end{equation}
where $\bar n$ is the mean count over the building types with at least ten copies. A purchase raises the expected income by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta-bar}
 \bar\Delta_i=(1+\sigma_c)\,\Delta_i+(1+\sigma_k)\,r\,\delta_i.
\end{equation}
Golden cookies bring in
\begin{equation}\label{eq:golden-income}
 G=\sigma_c\,c+\sigma_k\,k+\lambda_L\,g\min(0.15\,b,\ 900\,c)
\end{equation}
in all. The expected income, which fills the bank, is $c+k+G$.
\end{definition}

Frenzies and building specials multiply CpS, and so also the click power that comes from it. Below the cap, \Lucky{} pays a share of the bank, not of production, so it is absent from \eqref{eq:delta-bar}.

The golden-cookie upgrades follow from \eqref{eq:golden-income}. \emph{Lucky day} and \emph{Serendipity} double the frequency, so each is worth $G$. \emph{Get lucky} doubles every duration and is worth $\sigma_c\,c+\sigma_k\,k$. \emph{Golden goose egg} adds $5\%$ frequency and is worth $0.05\,G$.

With these frozen income estimates, the same algebra gives a pairwise comparison and a marginal bank comparison. Replacing random income by its mean does not preserve expected hitting times in general. Both use the income without current buffs. Otherwise a Frenzy would make every purchase look seven times better for its 77 seconds. The heavenly-upgrade values of Table~\ref{tab:heavenly} use the simpler shares
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma-simple}
 \sigma_F=\frac{462\,\pi_F\,d}{\E[I]},\qquad\sigma=\sigma_F+\frac{\pi_L\,g\min(900,\ 0.15\,b/c)}{\E[I]},
\end{equation}
which count Frenzies and \Lucky{} only.

\section{Heavenly chips}\label{sec:chips}
%======================================================================
Chips arrive over the course of the game. Earning the chips between the budget levels $B$ and $B+dB$ takes the time
\[
 \frac{q(B)}{S(B)}\,dB,
\]
where $q$ is the number of cookies that each chip costs and $S(B)$ is the speed. The cost $q$ does not depend on the purchases. Heavenly upgrade $j$ costs $p_j$ chips and multiplies $S$ by $m_j>1$. The prestige factor \eqref{eq:multiplier} is common to all orders, so $q$ absorbs it.

\begin{theorem}[Ratio rule for multiplicative speed-ups]\label{thm:smith}
Suppose that all listed upgrades must be acquired, the initial chip bank is zero, there are no parent constraints, and $q>0$ is constant over the budget range. Prices are positive and multipliers exceed one. Then the order of purchases that minimises the total time sorts the items by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ratio}
 \rho_j=\frac{1-1/m_j}{p_j}
\end{equation}
in decreasing order. A swap of an adjacent pair strictly improves any order that is not sorted.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\Pi$ be the product of the multipliers bought before two adjacent items $i$ and $j$. A swap leaves every other term unchanged, because after both items the product is $\Pi m_im_j$ in either order. Let $S_0$ be the initial speed. The contribution of the pair is
\[
 \frac q{S_0\Pi}\Bigl(p_i+\frac{p_j}{m_i}\Bigr)\quad\text{with $i$ first},\qquad
 \frac q{S_0\Pi}\Bigl(p_j+\frac{p_i}{m_j}\Bigr)\quad\text{with $j$ first}.
\]
Hence $i$ first is strictly better if and only if
\[
 p_i+\frac{p_j}{m_i}<p_j+\frac{p_i}{m_j}
 \iff p_i\Bigl(1-\frac1{m_j}\Bigr)<p_j\Bigl(1-\frac1{m_i}\Bigr)
 \iff\rho_i>\rho_j.
\]
An unsorted order contains an adjacent inverted pair, and its swap strictly reduces the total time. There are finitely many orders, so a sorted order is optimal.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:chips}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
\item For small effects $1-1/m\approx\ln m$, so the rule reduces to the log-gain per chip. For large effects the two differ: \emph{Heralds} can be worth $m=2$.
\item The rule implies \emph{saving up}. A cheaper item with a lower ratio, bought first, delays an item with a higher ratio, and the interchange argument shows that this is worse. Cookie~Monster+ therefore spends chips along the sorted order and stops at the first unaffordable item.
\item A fixed ordered block has multiplier $M=\prod_{k=1}^r m_k$ and effective duration
\[
 d=\sum_{k=1}^r\frac{p_k}{\prod_{\ell<k}m_\ell}.
\]
Its exchange density is $(1-1/M)/d$, not $(1-1/M)/\sum p_k$. The latter ignores the speed gained from parents inside the block. For two independent blocks, the compared times are $d_A+d_B/M_A$ and $d_B+d_A/M_B$. This is a block exchange result, not a solution for all precedence graphs. The mod searches ancestor closures and preserves their internal order. Its block choice remains heuristic. Scheduling decompositions provide related ideas~\cite{sidney,lawler}.
\item The game awards chips at discrete ascensions and often starts with a nonzero chip bank. Chip effort changes with prestige. Those mechanics violate assumptions of the ratio theorem. A saving rule derived from that theorem is therefore a gameplay heuristic.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\section{When to ascend}\label{sec:ascension}
%======================================================================
\subsection{Time rescaling}\label{sec:rescale}
\begin{lemma}[Time rescaling]\label{lem:rescale}
Assume \ref{as:piecewise}, \ref{as:speed} and \ref{as:scalefree}. If a run at speed $1$ bakes $F(x)$ cookies in the time $x$, then the same run at speed $S$ bakes $F(St)$ cookies in the time $t$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $q(t)$ be the holdings and $r(q)$ the base income. At speed $S$ the bank obeys $\dot b=S\,r(q)$ between purchases. By \ref{as:scalefree}, purchase decisions depend only on $(b,q)$. In the time variable $\tilde t=St$ the dynamics are $db/d\tilde t=r(q)$, with the same decision rule, independent of $S$. Hence the path $(b,q)(\tilde t)$ is the same for every $S$, and the cookies baked, $\int r\,d\tilde t$, equal $F(\tilde t)$.
\end{proof}

Ranking purchases by payback period satisfies \ref{as:scalefree}, since every $\mathrm{PP}_i$ scales by $1/S$. Golden cookies satisfy it only approximately, because their spawn clock runs in real time. So does the \Lucky{} reserve, which scales with $S$.

\subsection{Formulation}\label{sec:formulation}
Run $k$ starts with the all-time cookies $A_k$ and the speed $S_k=S(P(A_k))$. If it lasts $T_k$, it bakes $C_k=F(S_kT_k)$ cookies by Lemma~\ref{lem:rescale}, and $A_{k+1}=A_k+C_k$. The player chooses the run lengths to minimise the total time $\sum_k(T_k+\tau)$ needed to reach a distant goal $A^\ast$. This is an optimal stopping problem with the Bellman equation~\cite{bellman}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bellman}
 V(A)=\min_{A<A'\le A^\ast}\Bigl[T(A\to A')+\tau\,\mathbf 1[A'<A^\ast]+V(A')\Bigr],\qquad V(A^\ast)=0.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Late game: a closed form}\label{sec:late}
In the late game $M(P)\approx0.01\,hP$, so $S(P)=\kappa P$. Take $\tau=0$ and assume~\ref{as:power}. Let $y=\ln P$. A run whose log-prestige grows by $\Delta$ must bake $C=10^{12}P^3(e^{3\Delta}-1)$ cookies. At speed $\kappa P$ this takes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:runtime}
 T(y,\Delta)=\frac{(C/\alpha)^{1/\beta}}{\kappa P}=K\,e^{\gamma y}\,\varphi(\Delta),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:runtime-parts}
 \varphi(\Delta)=(e^{3\Delta}-1)^{1/\beta},\qquad\gamma=\frac3\beta-1,\qquad K=\frac{(10^{12}/\alpha)^{1/\beta}}\kappa.
\end{equation}
When $\gamma>0$ ($\beta<3$), runs become longer as prestige grows. When $\gamma<0$, they become shorter.

\begin{theorem}[Lower bound and optimal step]\label{thm:lower}
Let $G(\Delta)=(e^{\gamma\Delta}-1)/\gamma$, with $G(\Delta)=\Delta$ if $\gamma=0$, and let
\[
 w=\inf_{\Delta>0}\frac{K\varphi(\Delta)}{G(\Delta)}.
\]
Every ascension schedule from $y_0$ to $y^\ast$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower}
 \sum_kT_k\ge\frac w\gamma\bigl(e^{\gamma y^\ast}-e^{\gamma y_0}\bigr)
 \qquad\bigl(\text{or }w\,(y^\ast-y_0)\text{ if }\gamma=0\bigr),
\end{equation}
with equality if and only if every step $\Delta_k$ minimises $\varphi/G$. For $\beta>1$ a minimiser $\Delta^\star$ exists.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\proofstep{Bound.} For $\gamma\ne0$ and $\Delta>0$, $G(\Delta)>0$ and
\[
 e^{\gamma y}G(\Delta)=\frac{e^{\gamma(y+\Delta)}-e^{\gamma y}}\gamma.
\]
By the definition of $w$, $K\varphi(\Delta)\ge w\,G(\Delta)$ for every $\Delta>0$. With $y_{k+1}=y_k+\Delta_k$ the sum telescopes:
\[
 \sum_kT_k=\sum_kKe^{\gamma y_k}\varphi(\Delta_k)\ge w\sum_ke^{\gamma y_k}G(\Delta_k)
 =\frac w\gamma\sum_k\bigl(e^{\gamma y_{k+1}}-e^{\gamma y_k}\bigr)=\frac w\gamma\bigl(e^{\gamma y^\ast}-e^{\gamma y_0}\bigr).
\]
The only inequality is an equality exactly when $K\varphi(\Delta_k)=w\,G(\Delta_k)$ for every $k$. The case $\gamma=0$ is the same with $G(\Delta)=\Delta$.

\proofstep{Existence.} As $\Delta\to0^+$,
\[
 \frac{\varphi(\Delta)}{G(\Delta)}\sim3^{1/\beta}\Delta^{1/\beta-1}\to\infty,
\]
because $\beta>1$. As $\Delta\to\infty$, $\varphi(\Delta)\sim e^{3\Delta/\beta}$, while $G\sim e^{\gamma\Delta}/\gamma$ for $\gamma>0$, $G=\Delta$ for $\gamma=0$, and $G\to-1/\gamma>0$ for $\gamma<0$. Since $\gamma<3/\beta$, the ratio tends to infinity in all three cases. A continuous function on $(0,\infty)$ that tends to infinity at both ends attains its minimum.
\end{proof}

Repeating $\Delta^\star$ achieves the bound only when the goal lies on that sequence of steps. A final partial step can create a gap. Adjusting several steps can improve a finite-goal schedule, so the constant-step rule is not an exact solution for every endpoint. For $\beta\le1$ the ratio $\varphi/G$ has no interior minimum, and resets should be as frequent as the overhead allows. This degenerate case motivates a safeguard in the implementation (Section~\ref{sec:dp}).

\begin{corollary}[Stopping rule]\label{cor:stop}
At an interior minimiser, $\varphi'/\varphi=G'/G$. The current run's length is proportional to $\varphi(\Delta)$, so a step that attains the lower bound satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stop-rate}
 \frac{d\Delta}{dT}=\frac{1-e^{-\gamma\Delta}}{\gamma\,T}.
\end{equation}
For a run whose cookies grow like $T^\beta$, $\Delta=\tfrac13\ln v$ with $v=(A+C)/A$. A step attaining that bound has a factor $v^\star>1$ that solves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vstar}
 \frac\beta3\Bigl(1-\frac1v\Bigr)=\frac{1-v^{-\gamma/3}}\gamma.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
From $T'(\Delta)/T(\Delta)=\varphi'/\varphi=G'/G$,
\[
 \frac{d\Delta}{dT}=\frac G{G'\,T}=\frac{e^{\gamma\Delta}-1}{\gamma e^{\gamma\Delta}\,T}.
\]
With $C\propto T^\beta$, $dv/dT=\beta(v-1)/T$, so $d\Delta/dT=\tfrac1{3v}\,dv/dT=\tfrac\beta3(1-1/v)/T$. Equating the two expressions gives \eqref{eq:vstar}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The marginal value theorem]\label{rem:mvt}
For $\gamma=0$ ($\beta=3$) the rule reads $d\Delta/dT=\Delta/T$: ascend when the marginal rate equals the average rate. This is the marginal value theorem of optimal foraging~\cite{charnov}. Its use at $\beta\ne3$ ignores that later runs become longer or shorter. It then solves
\[
 \beta(v-1)=v\ln v
\]
instead of \eqref{eq:vstar}. The cookie-growth factors differ by $1.6$ at $\beta=2$ and $6.5$ at $\beta=6$ (Table~\ref{tab:vstar}).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Overhead]\label{rem:overhead}
With $\tau>0$ the problem is no longer self-similar. Corollary~\ref{cor:stop} then holds approximately, with $T$ replaced by $T+\tau$. Dynamic programming solves the stated finite-grid model exactly (Section~\ref{sec:dp}). Grid restriction and estimated run curves remain approximation errors.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Early game: why early resets fail}\label{sec:early}
\begin{proposition}[No prestige effect, no reset]\label{prop:noprestige}
Suppose that every run has the same speed, $S(P)\equiv S$, and that $F(x)=\alpha x^\beta$ with $\beta>1$. Then for every $\tau\ge0$, a single run is the fastest way to bake a given number of cookies.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Split $D$ cookies into runs of $D_1,\ldots,D_n$. The total time is
\[
 \sum_k\frac{(D_k/\alpha)^{1/\beta}}S+(n-1)\tau.
\]
Since $q=1/\beta\in(0,1)$, the map $x\mapsto x^q$ is strictly subadditive on $[0,\infty)$: $(\sum_kD_k)^q<\sum_kD_k^q$ for $n\ge2$ positive parts. Hence one run is strictly fastest.
\end{proof}

Early in the game, ascension raises the speed only slightly, so the problem is close to this case and early resets are costly. An earlier version of the advisor applied the late-game rule to a first run of $4.89\times10^{12}$ cookies: prestige $1.70$, one level, $+1\%$ production. It recommended an ascension. The planner below says to keep baking. It puts the cost of an ascension at that point at 11 to 16 hours of total progress (Table~\ref{tab:firstrun}).

\subsection{The general planner}\label{sec:dp}
\proofstep{Speed model.} A run that starts at prestige $P$ has the speed
\begin{equation}\label{eq:speed}
 S(P)=\Bigl(\prod_{j\in J(\floor P)}m_j\Bigr)\bigl(1+0.01\,h\floor P\bigr).
\end{equation}
Legacy is bought with the first chip. $J(\floor P)$ is the longest prefix of the order of Theorem~\ref{thm:smith}, parents included, that the chips available at $P$ pay for.

\proofstep{Run-time model.} The current run has baked $C$ cookies in the time $T$, and $\beta=cT/C$. A fresh run rebuilds to $C$ convexly and then grows like the current run:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:run-model}
 t(X)=\begin{cases}T\,(X/C)^{1/\beta_r},&X\le C,\quad\beta_r=\max(\beta,1.5),\\[2pt] T\,(X/C)^{1/\beta},&X>C.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
A run at speed $S$ needs $t(X)\,S_{\mathrm{now}}/S$ to bake $X$ cookies (Lemma~\ref{lem:rescale}). The floor $\beta_r\ge1.5$ states that every run starts from zero production, even when the current run has stalled.

\begin{proposition}[Exactness of the dynamic program]\label{prop:dp}
Restrict ascensions to a finite grid of all-time cookie levels $a_0<a_1<\cdots<a_n=A^\ast$, and let $t(i,j)$ be the duration of a run from $a_i$ to $a_j$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dp}
 V(i)=\min_{j>i}\Bigl[t(i,j)+\tau\,\mathbf 1[j<n]+V(j)\Bigr],\qquad V(n)=0,
\end{equation}
computed for $i=n-1,\ldots,0$ in $O(n^2)$ time, is the minimum total time from $a_i$. The minimising $j$ is the optimal next ascension point. For the current run, let $r_j$ be the remaining time to grid level $a_j$. Its value is
\[
 \min\left\{r_n,\ \min_{j<n}\bigl[r_j+\tau+V(j)\bigr]\right\}.
\]
The first term reaches the goal without a reset. It is required even when a reset would increase speed.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
All-time cookies only increase, so schedules are paths in a directed acyclic graph on the grid, with edge weights $t(i,j)+\tau\,\mathbf 1[j<n]$. The recursion is Bellman's equation~\eqref{eq:bellman} on this graph. By induction on $n-i$, $V(i)$ is the shortest path length from $i$ to $n$.
\end{proof}

Four implementation choices keep the planner robust.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Horizon.} The goal is the level that the current growth would reach in $10^4$ run lengths. A more distant goal would let floating-point error swamp the hour-scale differences of the current decision.
\item \emph{End effects.} The target is the median over five goals spaced over one decade of all-time cookies. This reduces sensitivity to the endpoint. The median of policies for different goals need not minimise any one objective.
\item \emph{Minimum gain.} An ascension must raise the speed by at least $10\%$. This guards against a stalled run, for example while nothing is bought. Such a run pushes $\beta$ toward $1$, where Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} favours constant resets.
\item \emph{Grid and age.} The grid has 400 log-spaced levels plus every whole prestige level below 400. No plan is made before a run is ten minutes old.
\end{itemize}

\section{The rest of the game}\label{sec:rest}
%======================================================================
The preceding results justify restricted comparisons, not a complete-game optimum. The game has further systems, and most of them reset at every ascension. Each system has a rule that uses the long-run income model of Definition~\ref{def:auto}, extended below to wrath cookies and wrinklers. This section states the facts that the rules rest on.

\subsection{Achievements}\label{sec:achievements}
\begin{proposition}[Value of an achievement]\label{prop:achievement}
Each achievement, shadow achievements aside, raises the milk $\mu$ by $0.04$. Let the kitten upgrades have the factors $k_i$, and let $m$ be the milk multiplier. CpS carries the factor $\prod_i(1+k_im\mu)$, so one more achievement multiplies CpS by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:achievement}
 \prod_i\frac{1+k_im(\mu+0.04)}{1+k_im\mu}\approx\exp\Bigl(0.04\sum_i\frac{k_im}{1+k_im\mu}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The game multiplies CpS by $1+k_im\mu$ for each kitten owned. Substitute $\mu+0.04$ and take the ratio. The approximation is $\ln(1+x)\approx x$ for each factor.
\end{proof}

On an observed save with eight kittens and $\mu=9.52$, one achievement adds $2.14\%$ of CpS. This return alone does not prove that every small achievement should be taken at once. Fifty news clicks or a viewport change can be cheap. Holding a golden cookie until its last second delays its reward; repeated wrinkler pokes need recovery time; and a self-gift has a native cooldown. These costs and any lost alternative action must enter the schedule. For the completion objective, each required achievement must eventually be won even when its production return is small.

\begin{proposition}[What an achievement is worth in time]\label{prop:worth}
Suppose a fixed goal takes $H\ge0$ more seconds without an action. The action pauses progress for $D\ge0$ seconds. It then multiplies all future rates by $M>0$, with unchanged paths. The action improves completion time if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:worth}
 D+\frac H M<H
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 D<H\left(1-\frac1M\right).
\end{equation}
For $M=1+x$, the saved time is $Hx/(1+x)$, not $Hx$. For $k$ milk achievements, use the combined factor
\[
 M=\prod_i\frac{1+k_i m(\mu+0.04k)}{1+k_i m\mu}.
\]
Adding $k$ copies of the first achievement's percentage is only a small-gain approximation.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The action has total completion time $D+H/M$ by time rescaling. Compare it with $H$ and rearrange. Substitute the new milk level for the combined factor.
\end{proof}

A cookie expense converted to $D=C/I$ is a further approximation when income changes. Save age is a chosen horizon estimate, not measured time to completion.

The horizon is unknown. A proper Pareto prior with survival proportional to $1/t$ gives a conditional median remaining time equal to elapsed time. A log-uniform density proportional to $1/t$ is improper without bounds and gives no such conclusion. Autoplay therefore takes $H$ to be the age of the save. On the observed save, after 77 hours, one achievement saves about $77(0.0214)/(1.0214)=1.61$ hours under this horizon estimate. Table~\ref{tab:actions} applies \eqref{eq:worth} to three kinds of action.

\subsection{Godzamok}\label{sec:godzamok}
Godzamok, one of the Pantheon's gods, rewards the sale of buildings. Each building sold nominally adds $b=1\%$ to the click power of a ten-second ``Devastation'' buff ($0.5\%$ in the second slot, $0.25\%$ in the third). Sales while the buff runs do not extend its duration. Repeated additions can stop changing its binary64 multiplier when the increment is too small.

\begin{proposition}[Godzamok round trips]\label{prop:godzamok}
Assume real arithmetic, geometric prices without free copies or integer rounding, unchanged price modifiers, and no sale hook that changes production. Sell all $n$ copies of a building whose next copy costs $p$, and buy them back in the same frame. Production does not change, and the round trip costs
\begin{equation}\label{eq:round-trip}
 (1-1.15\,\sigma)\,\frac{p\,(1-1.15^{-n})}{0.15},\qquad\sigma=0.25,
\end{equation}
which is $0.7125$ times the cost to buy the copies back. The round trip adds $bn$ to the multiplier of the buff, which is worth $b\,n\,K\,t$ cookies. Here $K$ is the click income without the buff and $t$ is the remaining buff time. If $U$ buildings are cycled in each of the $F=300$ frames of a buff, the click multiplier averages
\begin{equation}\label{eq:devastation}
 1+bU\,\frac{F+1}2\approx1+150\,bU.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A sale at the amount $j$ refunds $\sigma$ times the price of copy $j+1$, which is $1.15$ times the price of copy $j$. Summed over the top $n$ copies, the refund is $1.15\,\sigma$ times the buy-back cost, a geometric sum. Click power is linear in the multiplier of the buff, so a sale adds $bnK$ per second for the remaining $t$ seconds. Cycling $U$ buildings per frame makes the multiplier $1+bUk$ in frame $k$. Average over $k=1,\ldots,F$.
\end{proof}

Autoplay cycles building types whose estimated return is more than twice the round-trip cost. Version 2.053.17 projects the bank through every sale credit and purchase debit in native order. It checks affordability at each purchase and uses the resulting bank loss. Summing individually rounded prices is not enough: the bank operations also round. The projection assumes unchanged price modifiers. Version 2.053.21 also computes the immediate increase of a finite multiplier with native operation order. Put $b_{64}=\operatorname{RN}_{64}(b)$ for the stored slot constant. Then
\[
 \Delta(m,n)=\operatorname{RN}_{64}\!\left(
   \operatorname{RN}_{64}\!\left(m+\operatorname{RN}_{64}(nb_{64})\right)-m\right).
\]
It uses $\Delta(m,n)Kt$ in the return estimate. If an addition rounds away, this click benefit is zero. A cycle can still pass when separate rounded bank operations produce a net gain. The future-average forecast starts each proposed buff at multiplier one; it remains a heuristic. Boundary tests cover all three Pantheon slots and halfway ties. These supplied states do not prove new-save reachability. The mod retains its practical per-frame time and pass limits.

These limits are not restrictions in the zero-input-latency mathematical model. In a native diagnostic state with 101 Mines, Godzamok in the diamond slot, and bank $2^{90}$, selling and rebuying 100 Mines leaves the bank and holdings unchanged. All individual payments round away. One thousand such native UI cycles use zero modeled elapsed time and produce a multiplier of 1,001 without advancing its 300-frame duration. Repeating the unit increment has the recurrence
\[
 m_{n+1}=\operatorname{RN}_{64}(m_n+1),\qquad m_0=1,
 \qquad m_n=\min(n+1,2^{53}),
\]
where $\operatorname{RN}_{64}$ rounds to nearest binary64 with ties to even. Lean proves this recurrence for its integer rounding projection. A further transfer proves it for the actual binary64 multiplication and addition: selling exactly 100 buildings gives an increment of native one, and $2^{53}-1$ such additions from native one reach exactly $2^{53}$. Every finite prefix represents $\min\{k+1,2^{53}\}$, and another such increment leaves the cap unchanged. This arithmetic theorem does not supply the source sale/rebuy invariant. It also proves that, at bank $2^{90}$, every integer debit at most $2^{36}$ and refund at most $2^{37}$ leaves the bank unchanged, including halfway ties. At $2^{53}$, another unit is a tie and rounds back to $2^{53}$. Larger sale batches can produce larger increments, so this is not a global buff cap. The finite boundary probes do not execute the quadrillions of preceding cycles or establish a legal new-save route. Their significance is that a bounded per-frame search omits admissible mathematical schedules. A full optimality proof must account for those schedules, their native side effects, and their resource prerequisites. A separate finite controller now emits ordinary store inputs for the one-Cursor cent-increment route, with a bank prerequisite of $2^{60}$ and a deterministic bound of $2^{56}$ cycles. It maintains its own binary64 cent recurrence and does not read an exact native buff multiplier to choose its stopping point. Six supplied-boundary diagnostics execute 2\,181 cycles and check each native input; they do not execute the full bound or assign its endpoint. The controller leaves one Cursor and retains actual sale proceeds. Complete source and public-input correspondence, finite host-resource behavior, later income and restoration costs remain open. It is not enabled in the default candidate or mod.

The mouse upgrades make a click worth $15\%$ of CpS, so at 20 clicks a second $K\approx3c$ before the buff. On the observed save, about 2{,}200 buildings per frame kept the multiplier at $3{,}329$ on average. Income rose to about $22{,}000$ times CpS, from a few times CpS before. The game kept 30 frames a second.

The economy model includes the average multiplier \eqref{eq:devastation} in click income, so the Pantheon slots Godzamok as soon as a worship swap allows. Each ascension restores three swaps. Once they are spent, the next swap takes 16 hours. When a much better arrangement waits for swaps, autoplay keeps the next sugar lump for the refill that restores all three.

\subsection{Wrinklers and wrath cookies}\label{sec:wrath}
\begin{proposition}[Wrinkler factor]\label{prop:wrinklers}
With $n$ wrinklers digesting, a share $s=\min(1,n/20)$ of the CpS $c$ withers, and every wrinkler accumulates $sc$ per second. A popped wrinkler returns $r\ge1.1$ times its contents. Income from CpS is therefore $c\,W(n)$, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wrinklers}
 W(n)=1-s+s\,n\,r,\qquad W(10)=6,\qquad W(12)=8.32\qquad(r=1.1).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In the game's update, each digesting wrinkler adds $cs$ per second to its stomach: the whole withered share, not $1/n$ of it. The bank receives $(1-s)c$. Sum over the $n$ wrinklers and apply the return factor.
\end{proof}

Wrinklers appear only during the Grandmapocalypse. At stage $\ell\in\{1,2,3\}$, each golden cookie is a wrath cookie with probability $\ell/3$. Wrath cookies replace Frenzies with clots, which halve production for 66\,s, and ruin, which takes $\min(0.05\,b,600\,c)$. In return they bring Elder frenzies (production $\times666$ for 6\,s), cursed fingers (each click worth 10\,s of production for 10\,s) and cookie storms: about 105 golden cookies in 7\,s, each worth one to seven minutes of CpS. \begin{proposition}[Calm wins once clicks dominate]\label{prop:calm}
With the Elder Pact, a pledge costs at most $8^{14}\approx4.4\times10^{12}$ cookies and returns by itself when it runs out. The stage is thus a free choice between calm (stage 0, no wrinklers) and wrath (stage 3, $W(n_{\max})$). Take $d=2$, a mean of $\bar n=200$ copies per building type, $\E[I]=121$\,s and twelve wrinklers. Golden cookies then multiply click income by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:calm-clicks}
 1+\frac{(595+0.30\,\bar n)\,d}{\E[I]}=11.8\ \text{(calm)},\qquad 1+\frac{(428+0.12\,\bar n)\,d}{\E[I]}=8.5\ \text{(wrath)},
\end{equation}
and CpS income by $8.7$ (calm) and $62.8$ (wrath). Calm earns more if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:calm-threshold}
 \frac kc>\frac{62.8-8.7}{11.8-8.5}\approx16.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For clicks, Frenzies, building specials and Click frenzies bring $(595+0.30\,\bar n)\,d$ buff-seconds per golden cookie in calm. In wrath, Elder frenzies and Click frenzies, less clots, cursed fingers and debuffs, bring $(428+0.12\,\bar n)\,d$. The shares are those of Table~\ref{tab:wrath}. Divide by $\E[I]$ and add one. For CpS, the same buffs count $W$ times, and storms and cursed fingers pay directly. Compare $8.7\,c+11.8\,k$ with $62.8\,c+8.5\,k$.
\end{proof}

Without Godzamok, clicks bring about $3c$ and wrath wins. With him, clicks bring thousands of times $c$, and calm earns about $40\%$ more. It then runs the elder achievements below and revokes the covenant that they leave, which costs $5\%$ of CpS.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta-stage}
 \frac1\theta=\frac{0.15\,\pi_Lg+0.215\,\pi_{\mathrm{chain}}\,g-0.05\,\pi_{\mathrm{ruin}}}{\E[I]},
\end{equation}
so $\theta$ rises from about $1.9\times10^3$\,s at stage 0 to $3.3\times10^3$\,s at stage 3.

\proofstep{When to pop.} Wrinklers lose nothing by waiting, but their contents earn nothing until popped. Pop a wrinkler that holds $X$ cookies now, $T$ seconds before the ascension. Its cookies then grow to $X\,e^{\rho T}$ by the end, where $\rho=(\beta-1)/t$ is the income growth rate of the run. Its slot stays empty for the respawn time $\tau$, and meanwhile the other wrinklers each digest a twentieth less. The loss is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pop-loss}
 L=\bigl(0.05\,(2n-1)\,r-0.05\bigr)\,c\,\tau,
\end{equation}
and it compounds to $L\,e^{\rho T}$. Autoplay pops the fattest normal wrinkler when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pop}
 X>\frac{L\,e^{\rho T}}{e^{\rho T}-1}.
\end{equation}

\proofstep{Elder achievements.} With the Elder Pact owned, the pledge returns as soon as a signed covenant is revoked. Pledge, covenant and revoke, repeated five times, earn all three elder achievements in seconds for a negligible amount of cookies, instead of two and a half hours of peace.

\subsection{Minigames}\label{sec:minigames}
\begin{proposition}[Fixed-capacity casting frequency]\label{prop:grimoire}
Fix a magic cap $M>0$, a spell cost $0<C\le M$, and a regeneration constant $\kappa>0$. Assume continuous regeneration at rate $\kappa\sqrt m$, instantaneous casts and no other changes to magic. At zero magic, use the refill solution $\sqrt{m(t)}=\kappa t/2$ until the cap. Casting whenever magic reaches $M$ maximizes the asymptotic number of casts per unit time.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A refill from $a$ to $a+C$ takes
\[
 \int_a^{a+C}\frac{dm}{\kappa\sqrt m}=\frac2\kappa\bigl(\sqrt{a+C}-\sqrt a\bigr),
\]
which decreases in $a$. Define the potential $H(m)=2\sqrt m/\kappa$ and $D=H(M)-H(M-C)>0$. During regeneration, $H$ increases at rate one; at the cap it stays constant. Each cast from any legal magic value decreases $H$ by at least $D$. For every schedule with $n$ casts by time $T$, telescoping these changes gives
\[
 nD\le T+H(m_0)-H(m_T)\le T+H(m_0).
\]
Thus the limiting upper cast frequency is at most $1/D$, including for nonperiodic schedules. Full-capacity casting attains this frequency, with one cast every $D$ time units after the initial refill.
\end{proof}

With $M\approx72$ and Force the Hand of Fate at $C\approx53$, a cast at full magic gives one cast every 23 minutes instead of every 40.
These figures use the continuous approximation. The native game regenerates in discrete updates, applies binary64 rounding and a positive minimum rate, and changes spell prices when the magic cap changes. Tower purchases, sales, sugar refills and ordinary ascensions also change the available schedule. The proposition does not minimize time to a fixed spell-count goal or to full-game completion. For example, if only one cast remains and current magic already covers its cost, waiting for the cap delays that goal.

The revised mod uses remaining magic immediately before an already-planned ordinary ascension. After cash-in, loans and the Lucky prestige wait, it casts the cheaper affordable spell from Haggler's Charm and Diminish Ineptitude until the 999-cast achievement is won or neither is affordable. Both native outcomes increase the spell count without changing cookies or lumps, and Reset clears their temporary buffs. The setting, challenge and transition guards remain active. This adds no reset and no regeneration wait. Eleven regression cases include both outcomes of both source spells and the final ascension gates. This is a count-progress heuristic, not a full-game dominance or optimality theorem; seeded spell state and later observations still depend on the cast history.

A separate experimental completion phase uses ordinary resets to obtain native magic at a fixed cap. With Wizard tower level 1, Reset sets the zero-tower cap and current magic to 5. Buying one tower retains that cap, and Conjure Baked Goods costs 4. Either native outcome increments the persistent spell counter. The candidate casts once, then resets on the same schedule after either outcome. It does not cancel a child timer or search for a favorable seed. A continuous source audit from a declared $10^{57}$-cookie, one-lump entry completes 999 casts in 5\,335\,760 milliseconds: 859 successes and 140 backfires, with no later resource injection or extra lump spending. These zero-profit resets leave the profitable-reset counter unchanged. The controller therefore requires the separate 100-reset milestone and growth goals first, and finishes its last reincarnation before final Garden and market collection. This is a conditional source witness, not a proved new-save route or a uniform expected-time bound.

\proofstep{Pantheon.} Three slots take three of eleven gods. Autoplay evaluates every ordered triple of the useful gods with the game's CpS formula and the income model. The golden-cookie penalties of Mokalsium and Jeremy enter through the interval. Autoplay moves gods only when income rises by more than $1\%$ and worship swaps are available.

\proofstep{Garden.} Most new plant kinds appear by mutation in empty tiles next to the required parents. Some recipes accept immature parents. Meddleweed can appear without parents; uprooting old meddleweed can release brown mold or crumbspore. Harvesting a mature plant normally unlocks its seed. Pebbles can also unlock a seed when the plant dies of old age.

\begin{lemma}[Checkerboard breeding]\label{lem:checkerboard}
On a rectangular plot of at least $2\times2$ tiles, plant the parent kind $A$ on the tiles with $x+y$ even and $x$ even, and the kind $B$ on the tiles with $x+y$ even and $x$ odd. Leave the tiles with $x+y$ odd empty. Then every empty tile touches both kinds (two parents of kind $A$ when $A=B$), so every empty tile can mutate into a seed bred from $A$ and $B$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
An empty tile $(x,y)$ has $x+y$ odd. Its horizontal neighbours $(x\pm1,y)$ are parent tiles with the parity of $x+1$, and its vertical neighbours $(x,y\pm1)$ are parent tiles with the parity of $x$. In a plot at least two tiles wide and tall, at least one tile of each pair lies inside the plot.
\end{proof}

Autoplay breeds along the chains whose parents can be planted: Thumbcorn from wheat, then Cronerice, Gildmillet, Clover, Shimmerlily, Elderwort, Golden clover and Bakeberry. It uproots whatever else sprouts in the empty tiles. It lets each new kind grow up and picks it when mature, which unlocks its seed. Wood chips (three mutation rolls per tick, from 300 farms), or else fertilizer (a tick every three minutes instead of five), speed this up.

Thumbcorn, at $+2\%$ click power, is worth $2\%$ of nearly all income once Godzamok multiplies clicks. Golden clover, at $+3\%$ golden-cookie frequency, is worth $3\%$ of the golden-cookie income. The plot is cleared at every ascension, so breeding advances only in runs long enough for the parents to mature.

With nothing left to breed, autoplay plants a seed $j$ on clay (effects $\times1.25$). Let the seed add $v_j$ to long-run income over the mature lifetime $T_j$, for the price $p_j$. It is planted when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:plant}
 v_jT_j>p_j\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{p_j}{v_j}<\text{payback of the next purchase}.
\end{equation}
Plants are harvested while mature, just before the growth tick that could kill them, which counts toward the harvest achievements. One tile stays empty for mutations.

\subsection{The dragon, seasons, sugar lumps and permanent slots}\label{sec:other}
\proofstep{Dragon.} Sacrificing $n$ copies of a building whose next copy costs $p$ costs
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rebuy}
 p\,\frac{1-1.15^{-n}}{0.15}\approx6.67\,p\qquad(n=100)
\end{equation}
to buy back. The dragon starts over every run. Radiant Appetite doubles production and needs the sacrifice of 100 copies of each of the first 14 buildings. Training toward it goes ahead once all those buildings are at hand and buying them back costs at most an hour of income. The same rule holds for Dragon Guts, which adds two wrinklers that digest and return $20\%$ more, so that $W$ reaches about $11.7$. Auras are chosen by evaluation, and a change costs one top building.

\proofstep{Seasons.} Christmas brings the 14 levels of Santa, which cost
\begin{equation}\label{eq:santa}
 \sum_{j=1}^{14}j^j\approx1.2\times10^{16}
\end{equation}
cookies in total, with a gift at each level (Santa's legacy gives $+3\%$ per level). It also brings reindeer, each worth one to two minutes of CpS. Valentine's day brings seven heart biscuits, $+2\%$ each, and Easter brings eggs. Each run visits Christmas until Santa is complete, Valentine's day once the hearts cost a minute of production, and Easter until seven eggs while that achievement is missing. Each switch costs $1.5$ times the one before, so the run waits in Christmas for cheap hearts and stays in Valentine's day until the last heart. It otherwise stays in Christmas. The auto-buyer keeps up to a minute of production for the next seasonal action. A purchase that pays back within a minute goes first, because it delays the action by less than a minute.

\proofstep{Sugar lumps.} Lumps go first to the minigames and then to the Sugar frenzy of each run, once Sugar craving is owned: production $\times3$ for an hour, two hours of income for one lump. Next come cursor levels until the first loan opens, 11 lumps counting the bank's own level, for $+50\%$ CpS in the last two hours of every run. The rest go to building levels. A level of building $b$ adds $1\%$ of its CpS, a share
\begin{equation}\label{eq:level}
 \frac{0.01\,s_b}{1+0.01\,\ell_b}
\end{equation}
of CpS, for $\ell_b+1$ lumps, plus the value \eqref{eq:achievement} at level 10. Levels are bought by gain per lump, saving up for the best one as in Remark~\ref{rem:chips}. The mature but unripe Hand-picked attempt is taken once the minigames are open. Its nominal failure probability is one half under a fresh uniform draw. Section~\ref{sec:source-randomness} explains why this is not an unconditional model of every legal interaction.

\proofstep{Permanent slots.} Milk carries over, so a kitten kept in a slot multiplies CpS by $1+km\mu$ from the first second of each run, and a cookie upgrade by $1$ plus its power. The slots take last run's upgrades in decreasing order of the logarithm of that multiplier. These systems also enter the heavenly-upgrade values, as estimates: How to bake your dragon $1.8$, Season switcher $1.3$, permanent slots $1.12$ down to $1.03$, Elder spice $1.25$, Unholy bait $1.05$, Sacrilegious corruption $1.04$ and Wrapping paper $1.02$.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:implementation}
%======================================================================
The rules run in Cookie~Monster+~\cite{cmplus}, a fork of the Cookie Monster mod~\cite{cm} for the Steam version of the game (2.053). Every second the mod records the ascension inputs. Every five seconds it solves the dynamic program again. Earlier latency claims lacked reproducible traces and are not used as validation here. With automation on, the mod also does the following.
\begin{itemize}
\item It clicks the big cookie 20 times per second. The game ignores clicks less than 20\,ms apart. The browser throttles timers in a hidden window, and the game makes up at most five seconds per run of its loop. While the window is hidden, a worker's timer therefore runs the missed logic frames and clicks, with the game's own catch-up.
\item It clicks every golden cookie and reindeer as soon as it appears.
\item In every frame with Godzamok slotted, it sells and buys back the building types of Proposition~\ref{prop:godzamok} within three milliseconds. The counts are restored before the frame ends, so only click power is computed again.
\item In every frame, it buys items in order of payback period, computed with the automation values of Section~\ref{sec:golden} and the \Lucky{} reserve of Proposition~\ref{prop:bank}. It skips switches, sugar-lump costs and the Chocolate egg. A frame uses the values from its start for at most four milliseconds, and it buys consecutive copies of a building in one call. More copies of a building only raise the values of other purchases, so these values never overrate a purchase. The frame stops after an upgrade, and before a second purchase that needs a reserve, because the \Lucky{} bank grows with CpS. Cookie Monster recomputes the values on the next frame. Earlier versions bought one item every ten frames, which left the start of each run far behind its bank.
\item On nine of every ten frames, it also runs one of the systems of Section~\ref{sec:rest}: the Grandmapocalypse (stage choice, sprees and wrinklers), the dragon, seasons, sugar lumps, the Pantheon, Grimoire, Garden and Stock Market, and the achievements that only take an action.
\item Once the planner has said ``ascend now'' for ten seconds, it pops wrinklers, waits for their payouts while other spending is paused, buys the Chocolate egg and ascends. The payout forecast includes Dragon Guts. Version 2.053.15 checks pending payouts instead of adding a fixed two-second wait. It sends the native Escape input after its own ascent request; this ends the five-second ascent animation on the next frame. It also removes the fixed 1.5-second wait on the heavenly screen. It spends chips along the order of Theorem~\ref{thm:smith}, parents first and saving up for the next item. It fills the permanent slots and reincarnates, into a Born again run when Proposition~\ref{prop:worth} says that the challenge pays. It ascends early when a loan is about to reach its interest.
\end{itemize}
Version 2.053.20 retains the native click-sound callback during muted auto-clicks. Earlier versions replaced that callback and skipped its random draw and sound-index update. The new implementation temporarily uses zero sound volume and restores volume and pointer fields after the native click, including an exceptional return. Paired source tests retain the same economic state, sound index and subsequent random output under both free and seeded generators. They also check the native rejection of a repeated same-time click.

The regression suite checks the no-reset alternative, combined achievement gains, discounted block costs, affordable chip prefixes, safe buybacks, and payout ordering. An integration test runs the actual ascent controller against the pinned engine. It checks that wrinkler payouts precede the egg purchase and that the native Escape event advances the ascent. Tests of a model do not prove equivalence with the game. Section~\ref{sec:formal} distinguishes kernel-checked statements from JavaScript tests.

\section{Formal verification and audit scope}\label{sec:formal}
%======================================================================
The \texttt{lean/} project contains the machine-checked statements. Its README gives the pinned Lean version, build command, theorem names, and exact hypotheses. The reference completion solver is \texttt{tools/completion-model.mjs}. It uses exact rational arithmetic for finite, fixed purchases. The regression suite includes Example~\ref{ex:greedy} and exhaustive small-instance comparisons.

The main deterministic result is \texttt{complete\_global\_optimal}. It proves attainment and comparison against every affordable rational-time completion schedule for the fixed purchase list. Nonnegative gains and positive initial income are required. The result includes arbitrary extra waits. The theorem \texttt{greedy\_counterexample} checks the exact counterexample, and \texttt{achievement\_break\_even} checks the corrected delay inequality.

The stochastic result \texttt{Stochastic.certified\_optimal} uses finite probability laws and policies with a finite depth bound. Each action has a nonnegative duration. Probabilities are nonnegative rationals that sum to one. Every policy leaf must satisfy the supplied completion predicate. A value function that bounds every legal action from below, with equality along an attaining policy, proves optimal expected time among those policies. A three-state example checks an expected optimum of $5/2$ seconds. No unfinished leaf receives a free terminal cost.

The generated Lean completion predicate uses the Steam catalogue and the explicit terminal profile from Section~\ref{sec:completion-objective}. Checked statements show that missing a required item prevents completion and that shadow flags do not change completion. The exact partition of the 622 normal achievements also proves that completion requires at least two distinct won achievements from the 24 individual sugar exceptions. A synthetic terminal witness checks consistency of the predicate; it does not establish reachability from a new save. Generation checks detect stale identifiers. They do not prove equivalence between the JavaScript implementation and the Lean function.

The Garden trace theorem proves a further necessary condition. From an initial state without Juicy queenbeet or Seedless to nay, full completion requires at least two acquisitions of the Juicy queenbeet seed and two distinct states with a complete seed log. Its transition premise requires the first Seedless award to follow a complete log and to reset that seed. The trace checker tests this premise on supplied adjacent observations. Native tests separately exercise sacrifice, mature harvest, and pebbles unlocking a seed on old-age death. Consequently, the theorem does not incorrectly require two manual harvests. It remains conditional on the trace contract; no proof covers all executions of the JavaScript source.

The age audit gives a finite candidate state component. Native growth adds an integer from \texttt{randomFloor}. Mortal ages remain integers below 100 between updates. The two immortal plants have caps 91 and 76, reduced to 86.5 and 72.25 after Seedless to nay. Lean checks those caps with the actual binary64 operations. For the integer-growth projection, it proves closure of the 102-value domain consisting of integers 0--99 and the two fractional caps. A projection that keeps mortal and non-mature immortal ages, but groups mature immortal ages, is preserved by equal growth outcomes and by current-save truncation. The full native growth bound, helper execution and heap correspondence remain proof obligations.

This age projection does not identify complete public observations. The native Elderwort growth indicator is at 221 pixels for age 86.5 and at 220 pixels after save truncation to 86. Lean checks that difference; 3\,612 native source-fragment fixtures check the growth and clamp operations with supplied integer growth outcomes. A full-game reduction must retain the extra observation or prove that it cannot improve economic control. A finite list of age values alone does not prove an optimal strategy exists.

The unbounded stochastic layer compares the suprema of expected accumulated costs of finite truncations. Its verification rule requires local Bellman inequalities, equality for the proposed policy, and a vanishing expected residual potential for finite-cost alternatives. This removes the finite-depth restriction on that cost theorem. It is still conditional on the supplied transition system and certificate. It also needs a separate connection to completion time: an infinite sequence of zero-duration actions can have zero accumulated cost while never reaching the target. The formal examples expose this issue rather than assign zero completion time to an unsuccessful path.

The separate \texttt{HittingTime} construction defines completion time directly from execution prefixes. For a cap $H$ and prefix depth $n$, a completed path contributes $\min(T,H)$, and an unfinished prefix contributes $H$. Write the resulting exact finite expectation as $C_{H,n}$. Depth increases can only decrease this quantity. The completion-time value is represented by the rational lower cut of
\[
 \sup_{H\in\mathbb N}\inf_{n\in\mathbb N} C_{H,n}.
\]
The order of these limits matters. Taking a supremum over unfinished depth penalties would give a false infinite value before a successful zero-time retry. The checked construction gives infinite time to a permanent zero-time stall and zero expected additional time to the known-missing pet retry. Its finite transition programs can consume an adaptive number of independent 53-bit draws. Deterministic seeded calculations remain part of hidden state. A policy receives its public observation and action history. Random startup is averaged before that policy acts, and legality is required only on reachable unfinished histories. These definitions do not supply the game's transition program or its global optimality certificate.

The randomized-controller extension carries private memory and permits fresh independent random choices at every decision. It places no bound on the total number of decisions or private draws. A capped full-state function $V_H$ is a lower certificate if it is at most $H$, is at most the capped elapsed time in every complete state, and satisfies $V_H(s)\leq\mathbb{E}[V_H(S')\mid s,a]$ for every legal action in an unfinished state. Lean proves that this certificate bounds every finite-prefix expectation of each controller, then the corresponding expected-time lower cut. Deterministic public controllers retain exactly their previous values, and a permanent zero-time stall still has infinite expectation. Each individual decision sampler and native transition must terminate; unrestricted internal divergence remains outside this extension. No such certificate has yet been established for the full game's transitions. A lower bound may use a larger competitor class, but an attaining policy must separately satisfy the ban on timing chosen to change seeded outcomes.

The theorem \texttt{conditional\_hand\_picked\_bound} uses a rational survival-tail certificate at integer times. It verifies the finite-tail argument of Section~\ref{sec:source-randomness} and the conditional bound of 128\,872\,038 milliseconds. The concrete \texttt{SugarClock} transition system covers unripe and ripe harvests, failure resets, automatic harvests, initial activation, ordinary lump gains and spending, seeded type changes, offline whole-interval advances, and resets without rollback. It proves minimum attempt spacing and the inclusion of the no-success event in the no-completion event. The theorem \texttt{sugar\_run\_expected\_lower\_bound} uses these results to derive the clock bridge. Eleven native transition projections are separately checked by the Lean kernel, and the audit accounts for all seven source assignments to the lump timer. This finite test does not prove that every engine execution refines the transition system. Conditional failure, projection of actual runs, and finite-tail expectation soundness remain premises. Positive probability of noncompletion gives infinite hitting-time expectation. This is a necessary bound, not an optimality certificate.

The executable game adapter loads eight hash-pinned files, including the installed engine and all four minigames. It uses isolated storage, an explicit clock, a reproducible random stream, and a Steam interface fixture. Tests cover native click throttling, purchases, passive income, minigame loading, catalogue identity, and action replay. Startup now follows the inline page scripts, native load handler, language loader, launch, and filler-image callback. This restores a hidden top-bar draw and the console-greeting draw before seed creation. The default fixture fixes 100 heralds, English, zero asset latency, and a UTC start time. Its random tape is a reproducibility device, not a proof of a probability law. The normalized observation is not the full engine state.

A separate Lean source interpreter now makes JavaScript evaluation explicit. Closures contain syntax and lexical-environment indices; heaps, property descriptors, exception completions, and suspended callbacks are data. Its typed decoder accepts 153\,252 syntax nodes in 40 units from the pinned scripts and complete inline handlers. A decoded program is not yet a verified translation of its source bytes. Missing host operations remain unresolved model states. A runner fuel limit leaves an execution running; it does not turn model incompleteness into a game failure or an infinite completion time.

The interpreter represents ordinary numbers by their 64 bits and uses Lean's logical binary64 arithmetic. Decimal conversion and the required V8 mathematical functions have explicit implementations. Their 72\,384 finite comparisons use the installed Electron 11.5.0 engine, with V8 8.7.220.31. Unicode 13 tables supply UTF-16 casing and regular-expression character properties. Tests compare 11\,456 strings and all 1\,114\,112 Unicode code points for the three recorded properties. A source regular-expression matcher passes 947 native comparisons. \section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
%======================================================================
\begin{itemize}
\item The run curve is extrapolated from one number, the current growth exponent. Real runs accelerate when new tiers of upgrades unlock, and slow down when they run out. In simulation, the extrapolation of a decelerating run made the planner too patient by about $15\%$ in its first runs (Section~\ref{sec:numerics}).
\item The heavenly multipliers marked as estimates in Table~\ref{tab:heavenly} do not come from the game's code paths. Blocks restricted to ancestor closures are a heuristic for general precedence graphs.
\item The golden-cookie probabilities $\pi_F$ and $\pi_L$ are approximate. Assumption~\ref{as:scalefree} holds only approximately for golden cookies and the \Lucky{} reserve.
\item The analysis is risk-neutral. The rules of Section~\ref{sec:rest} rest on the income model and on simulated outcome shares. Several heavenly values, the Garden's seed values and the Stock Market thresholds are estimates.
\item The released mod's Garden heuristic covers only part of the seed catalogue. The separate experimental controller covers all 34 seeds and also has market and spell-count goals, but it does not implement a complete new-save route. The play horizon of Proposition~\ref{prop:worth} is a prior, not a measurement.
\item No full-game completion policy with a matching expected-time lower bound has been established. The necessary timing bounds do not prove attainment. A complete checked refinement of the installed game implementation remains open.
\item The simulator is a stylised economy. Its agreement with the theory supports the internal consistency of the rules, not their exact optimality in the real game.
\end{itemize}

\section{Algorithms}\label{app:algorithms}
%======================================================================
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Ascension planner (every five seconds)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State measure $A$, $C$, the run time $T$ and the income $c$; set $\beta\gets\min(\max(cT/C,0.3),12)$
\State build the chip order of Theorem~\ref{thm:smith}; define $S(P)$ by \eqref{eq:speed}
\For{each of five goals $a^\ast_k$ spaced over one decade beyond the $10^4$-run-length horizon}
  \State grid $\gets$ 400 log-spaced levels in $[A+C,a^\ast_k]$ $\cup$ whole prestige levels $<400$
  \State solve $V$ backwards by \eqref{eq:dp}; choose the target $j_k$ that minimises $\mathrm{remaining}(j)+\tau+V(j)$
  \State consider only targets with a speed gain of at least $10\%$
\EndFor
\State target $\gets$ median of $j_1,\ldots,j_5$; \textbf{ascend now} if and only if the target is the current level
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Automation tick (every frame; clicks on a separate 50\,ms timer)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State click every golden cookie and reindeer on screen
\If{the store is in buy mode and the values are current}
  \State for each candidate $i$: value $\bar\Delta_i$ by \eqref{eq:delta-bar}
  \Repeat
    \State reserve $R_i\gets$ the \Lucky{} bank if $p_i/\bar\Delta_i>\theta$, else $0$
    \State $i^\ast\gets\argmin_i\ \max(p_i-(b-R_i),0)/(c+k+G)+p_i/\bar\Delta_i$
    \State buy $i^\ast$ if $p_{i^\ast}\le b-R_{i^\ast}$, with the next copies of a building that pay back no slower than the runner-up
  \Until{nothing is affordable, 4\,ms have passed, an upgrade was bought, or a second purchase needs a reserve}
\EndIf
\If{the planner has said ``ascend now'' for 10\,s}
  \State pop wrinklers; wait for payouts; buy the Chocolate egg; ascend; spend chips along the order; reincarnate
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
